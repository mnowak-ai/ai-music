📚 Case Study

Two Artists Who Did Not Own Their Own Songs

One signed at nineteen in 1977. One signed at fifteen in 2005. Twenty-eight years apart, in completely different eras of the industry, they ended up saying the same sentence.

01

The Same Story, Twice

Prince signed with Warner Bros. Records in 1977, as a teenager. He went on to make 1999 and Purple Rain, some of the most important records ever made. Warner Bros. owned those master recordings, which is the actual audio of the actual performances.

Taylor Swift signed with Big Machine Records in 2005, at fifteen years old. She went on to make six albums that defined a generation of pop. Big Machine owned those masters.

Neither of them was tricked. Neither of them lacked representation. Both signed deals that were, by the standards of their time, fairly ordinary for a new artist. And both spent a significant portion of their careers trying to undo what those deals had set in motion.

02

The Clause That Rescued One of Them

Here is the detail most people miss when they hear about the "Taylor's Version" re-recordings, and it is the single most useful thing in this article.

She was able to re-record those albums because her contract's re-recording restriction had expired. Recording agreements almost always block an artist from re-recording their own songs, but that block usually runs for a set number of years. Once that window closed, nothing stopped her from recording those songs again and owning the new versions outright.

That is a clause. A boring, technical, easy-to-skim clause about timing. And it turned out to be the mechanism that let an artist build leverage against a catalog she did not own, until she was eventually able to buy the originals back in 2025.

Prince's situation had a similar hinge. Reporting on his 2014 settlement with Warner Bros. noted that it came as the 35-year copyright termination window on his earliest work was approaching, a provision in U.S. copyright law that can let creators reclaim rights decades after a transfer. Timing, again, buried in the legal machinery.

The lesson underneath both stories: the terms that decided these careers were not hidden. They were in the documents the whole time. What was missing, at fifteen and at nineteen, was a full understanding of what those specific words would mean twenty years later.

04

So Would AI Have Saved Them? Honestly, No.

It would be an easy story to tell, and it would be false. Let's be accurate about this, because it matters.

Neither artist lacked lawyers. These were represented deals. And AI did not exist in 1977, or in any usable form in 2005.

AI would not have given them leverage. This is the real constraint. A fifteen-year-old unknown songwriter and a nineteen-year-old unsigned musician have almost no bargaining power. Understanding a term perfectly does not mean you can get it changed. Sometimes the honest choice is signing an imperfect deal because it is the only door open, which is a legitimate decision when you make it knowingly.

So what does AI actually change? One thing, and it is not nothing:

It closes the gap between what a contract says and what a young artist understands it to say.

That gap used to cost money to close. You needed a lawyer's hour just to ask "wait, what does recoupable mean?" Today a teenager with a phone can get that translated in seconds, for free, at midnight, without feeling stupid for asking.

And knowing changes behavior. It changes which term you push back on, what you ask your lawyer to prioritize in the hour you can afford, whether you accept a smaller advance to keep your masters, and whether you notice that a re-recording clause runs out in seven years. Prince spent decades making the point that this knowledge should be common:

"If you don't own your masters, your master owns you." Prince, to Rolling Stone, 1996

That sentence is the entire curriculum of this article, delivered thirty years before you could ask a chatbot to explain it to you.

05

What This Means for You

You are not going to be offered a major label contract this week. But the pattern in both of these stories shows up in much smaller agreements, and those you will absolutely encounter: a producer wanting points on your song, a beat lease with terms you did not read, a manager agreement, a sync licensing deal, a battle-of-the-bands entry form that quietly claims rights to your recording.

The habit is what matters. Read it. Translate anything you do not understand. Write down your questions. Then bring those questions to a human whose job is to protect you.

Both of these artists eventually got their music back. It took Prince about thirty-seven years, and Taylor Swift twenty. You would rather understand the clause on day one.

Check Your Understanding

QUESTION 1
What are "master recordings"?
Masters are the original recorded audio. Whoever owns them controls how those specific recordings are used, licensed, and sold.
QUESTION 2
Which contract detail made the "Taylor's Version" re-recordings possible?
Re-recording restrictions usually last a set period. Once hers lapsed, she could legally record those songs again and own the new versions.
QUESTION 3
According to this article, what can AI genuinely change about signing a contract?
AI does not create leverage and does not replace legal counsel. What it does is make comprehension fast, free, and available to someone who cannot yet afford to ask a lawyer every question.

Key Takeaways

01

Prince signed with Warner Bros. in 1977 and Taylor Swift signed with Big Machine in 2005 as a teenager. In both cases the label owned the master recordings.

02

Prince protested by changing his name to a symbol and appearing with "slave" written on his face, and regained his masters in a 2014 deal with Warner Bros.

03

Swift re-recorded her early albums as "Taylor's Version" because a re-recording restriction had expired, and in May 2025 she bought back the original masters.

04

AI would not have given either artist leverage. What it offers today is instant comprehension, which is what tells you which term to fight for and what to ask a lawyer.

⚖️ Final reminder. AI does not replace a real lawyer or legal counsel. It has no license, no duty to protect your interests, and no accountability if it is wrong. Use it to understand a document and to prepare your questions. Use a qualified attorney to advise you and to decide. If cost is a barrier, look up Volunteer Lawyers for the Arts organizations, which provide free or low-cost legal help to artists in many states.
