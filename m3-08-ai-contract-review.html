AI for Contract Review

The day someone offers you a music contract is one of the best days of your life. It is also the day you get handed fourteen pages written in a language built to be hard to read.

Reading time: 9 min Interactive: 2 exercises Level: Beginner
01

The Scariest Document in Music

Here is how it usually goes. Someone likes your music. They send over an agreement. You are excited, you do not want to look difficult or inexperienced, and the document is full of phrases like "in perpetuity throughout the universe" and "fully recoupable from Artist's royalty account."

So a lot of people do the thing that ruins careers: they sign it without understanding it.

This is where AI has become genuinely useful, in a way that would have cost hundreds of dollars an hour ten years ago. Not to replace a lawyer. To make sure you walk into the lawyer's office already knowing what to ask.

The most important thing in that contract was one word: recoupable. An advance is not a gift, it is a loan against money you have not earned yet, and in many deals recording costs, video costs, and marketing all get charged back to your share. Plenty of artists have had a charting song and a royalty statement that still said zero. Understanding that single word changes how you read every other number in a deal.
02

What AI Is Genuinely Good At Here

Two things, and they map exactly to what you just did.

It translates. Paste a clause, ask what it means in plain English, and you get an answer instantly, at 2 a.m., without feeling embarrassed for not already knowing. That embarrassment is real and it is expensive, because it is the reason people sign things they do not understand.

It flags. Ask AI what in a document is unusual or unfavorable and it will point at the terms worth a second look: long option periods, ownership in perpetuity, revenue shares that reach beyond recorded music. It will not always be right, but it is very good at spotting what deserves a question.

The output you want is not "should I sign this." It is a list of questions. And the quality of what you get back depends almost entirely on how you ask.

04

Four Things AI Cannot Do

This is the section that matters most, so read it twice.

⚖️
It is not a lawyer
It has no license, no duty to protect your interests, and no responsibility if it is wrong. A lawyer who gives you bad advice can be held accountable. A chatbot cannot.
📍
It does not know where you are
Contract law differs by state and country. A clause that is unenforceable in one place is perfectly binding in another, and AI will rarely volunteer that distinction unless you ask.
🎭
It can be confidently wrong
AI states incorrect things in the same calm, authoritative tone it uses for correct ones. There is no wobble in its voice when it invents something. Treat every answer as a lead to verify, not a fact.
🔒
It does not know your leverage
Whether a term is acceptable depends on what you bring to the table and what else you have been offered. That is strategy, not language, and it needs a human who knows your actual situation.
One practical privacy warning. A contract has your legal name, and possibly your address, bank details, and terms the other side considers confidential. Before you paste any document into any AI tool, check what that tool does with what you give it, and consider removing personal details first. Some services train on what you type. Some have settings to turn that off. Know which one you are using.
05

You Can Probably Get a Real Lawyer for Free

Most artists assume legal help is out of reach financially. Often it is not.

Across the United States there is a network of organizations called Volunteer Lawyers for the Arts, which connect artists and musicians with attorneys who provide free or low-cost help specifically on things like contract review, copyright, and licensing. They exist in many states, they are used to working with people at the beginning of their careers, and reviewing a recording agreement is exactly the kind of thing they do.

Eligibility usually depends on income, and some charge a small application or membership fee, so check the details for your area. There is a national directory linked in the sources below. Many music schools, universities, and musicians' unions also offer contract help to members.

The combination that actually protects a young artist is this: use AI to understand the document and build your list of questions, then take that list to a human who is legally on your side.

The rule for this entire article
Use AI to understand a contract.
Use a human to decide whether to sign it.
06

Check Your Understanding

QUESTION 1
In a recording contract, what does "recoupable" mean?
Recoupable means an advance and certain costs are effectively a loan against your earnings. This is why an artist can have a popular song and still see zero on a royalty statement.
QUESTION 2
What is the best kind of output to ask AI for when reviewing a contract?
AI is strong at translating and flagging. Asking it to make the final call hands a licensed, accountable decision to a tool that is neither.
QUESTION 3
Why is "it can be confidently wrong" listed as a serious limitation?
There is no change in tone when AI is wrong. That is why every answer should be treated as a lead to verify rather than a settled fact.

Key Takeaways

01

AI can translate contract language into plain English instantly, which removes the embarrassment that makes people sign things they do not understand.

02

Watch for ownership in perpetuity, long option periods controlled only by the label, broad recoupment, and revenue shares that reach beyond recorded music.

03

Ask for translations, unusual terms, and questions for a lawyer. Never ask AI to make the decision for you.

04

Free and low-cost legal help for artists exists through Volunteer Lawyers for the Arts organizations. Use AI to prepare, then use a human to decide.

This article is educational material about how AI tools work, not legal advice. The contract shown is fictional and simplified, and real agreements vary enormously. What counts as a fair term depends on your situation, your leverage, and the law where you live. Always have an actual attorney review anything before you sign it.
