AI & Ethics

You now know how to use these tools. This article is about the harder question: when should you, and when should you stop? There is an old line about power arriving with responsibility. It applies here more than most places.

Reading time: 9 min Interactive: 2 exercises Level: Beginner
01

Nobody Is Going to Tell You the Rules

Here is the awkward reality of entering the music industry right now. The laws are being written while you work. Platform policies change every few months. Your teachers, your future manager, and the label down the street may all have different opinions about what is acceptable.

So you cannot wait for someone to hand you the rules. You need a set of your own, simple enough to actually use when you are tired and on a deadline and nobody is watching.

Three rules will carry you through almost every situation you will face.

RULE 01
Assist
AI works for you, not instead of you. Use it to speed up, sharpen, and support the work. The moment it is doing the thinking you were supposed to do, you have swapped a tool for a replacement.
Ask: am I still the one making this?
RULE 02
Verify
Check everything before it leaves your hands. AI states wrong things with exactly the same confidence it states right ones. Whatever you publish, you own, including the mistakes you did not catch.
Ask: do I actually know this is true?
RULE 03Respect Originality
Other people's work is not raw material. Do not pass off generated work as your own performance, and do not use AI to imitate a specific artist's voice or identity.
Ask: whose work is this really?

Simple to say. Much harder in the moment, because real situations are rarely clean. So let's practice on some that are not.

04

What "Respect Originality" Actually Means

This is the rule students ask the most questions about, because the boundary genuinely is blurry. Here is how to think about it.

Style is not owned. Identity is protected.

Nobody owns a genre, a chord progression, or a general vibe. Making a song that sounds like it belongs in the same world as music you love is what every artist in history has done. That is influence, and it is how music works.

What is different is using AI to copy a specific person: cloning a recognizable voice, generating a track marketed as sounding like a named living artist, or imitating someone's identity closely enough that listeners could be confused about who made it. That crosses from influence into impersonation, and it can carry real legal exposure around name, image, likeness, and voice rights.

Credit costs you nothing.

If AI helped, say so. Not in a self-flagellating way, just factually, the way you would credit a co-writer or a sample. The people who get hurt by AI disclosure are the ones who hid it and got found out. Nobody has ever damaged their career by being straightforward about their process.

Remember whose work trained the tool.

As you learned in the last article, the models behind these tools learned from enormous amounts of existing music, and whether that was fair is being fought over in courts right now. You do not have to resolve that debate to hold a simple position: the musicians whose work made these tools possible are real people, and pretending otherwise is the easiest way to end up on the wrong side of this.

A test that works when you are unsure: would you be comfortable if the person most affected by this could see exactly what you did? Not the public, not your teacher, the specific artist or listener on the other end. If the answer makes you wince, you already know.
05

Write Your Own AI Policy

Professionals in every creative field are writing these now. Yours can be five lines. Here is a starting draft you are welcome to adopt or argue with.

My AI Use Policy

  1. I use AI to assist, not to replace my judgment. If I could not explain and defend every choice in the finished work, I have gone too far.
  2. I verify anything factual before it leaves my hands. Names, dates, numbers, legal claims, and anything I would be embarrassed to get wrong in public.
  3. I disclose AI use plainly when it contributed meaningfully to something I release or submit.
  4. I do not imitate specific artists' voices or identities, and I do not present generated work as my own performance.
  5. I read the terms of any tool before I use its output commercially, and I do not assume that being allowed to use something means I own it.

Print it, save it, adapt it. The point is not the exact wording. The point is deciding what you stand for before you are in a situation where the wrong choice would be convenient.

Check Your Understanding

QUESTION 1
What does the "Assist" rule mean in practice?
The test is whether you are still the one making the work. If you could not explain and defend every choice, AI has moved from tool to replacement.
QUESTION 2
Why is "Verify" the rule people break most often without noticing?
There is no change in tone when AI is wrong. That is exactly why you cannot catch mistakes by reading carefully. You have to actually check.
QUESTION 3
Under "Respect Originality," what is the key difference between influence and impersonation?
Nobody owns a genre or a vibe. Copying a specific identifiable person's voice or identity is a different act, and can carry real legal exposure.

Key Takeaways

01

Assist: AI works for you, not instead of you. If you cannot explain every choice in the finished work, you have handed over too much.

02

Verify: check everything factual before you publish. Whatever goes out under your name is yours, including the mistakes you did not catch.

03

Respect Originality: general style is influence, copying a specific artist's voice or identity is impersonation. Disclose AI use plainly.

04

Decide what you stand for before the wrong choice becomes the convenient one, and write it down as your own short AI policy.
